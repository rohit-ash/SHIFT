Self-play is not enough: human imitation improves self-play autonomy in the wild

Self-play reinforcement learning at scale has become a powerful way to train autonomous driving policies. Agents that learn by driving against copies of themselves in simulation become very good at avoiding collisions and navigating stably, without ever seeing a human drive.

That last part is also the problem. A policy trained purely through self-play never sees how people actually drive, so its trajectories can look unnatural: it taps the brakes, oscillates, and makes choices that a passenger or a nearby driver would not expect. Robustness alone does not make a policy comfortable or predictable on a real road.

We present a hybrid training framework that fine-tunes a pretrained self-play policy on real-world human driving demonstrations, without changing the underlying reinforcement learning algorithm. The result is more comfortable, more human-like and safer in simulation, and the gains carry over to self-driving cars on public roads. To our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of self-play and self-play with human imitation on public roads.

How it works

imgs/figure1.png — training pipeline overview
Figure 1: Self-play pretraining followed by fine-tuning with human imitation in agent-on-rails simulation, regularized toward the frozen self-play policy.

We start from a policy pretrained with large-scale self-play using PPO with advantage filtering. Fine-tuning keeps that algorithm exactly as it is and changes three things around it.

Agent-on-rails simulation. We replay logged real-world driving scenes. The ego vehicle is rolled out by the learned policy, while every other agent is replayed from its logged human trajectory. The policy therefore practices in traffic that behaves the way real traffic did.

Human imitation as a reward term. The distance between the ego trajectory and the logged human expert trajectory is added as one more term in the reward. Nothing else about the RL objective changes, so fine-tuning reuses the same training stack as pretraining.

KL regularization. Fine-tuning for a long time on human data risks erasing what self-play taught the policy. We add a KL loss against the frozen pretrained self-play policy, which lets fine-tuning run for longer while keeping the robustness of the base policy.

Results in simulation

Compared to the self-play baseline, fine-tuning with human imitation improves every metric we track across comfort, human-likeness and safety. Brake taps drop by more than a factor of three, and the collision rate falls from 2.7% to 1.6%.

Looking ahead

Self-play gives a driving policy robustness, and human data gives it the behavior people expect. Our results suggest that post-training a self-play policy with human imitation, stabilized by KL regularization, is a promising direction for deployable autonomous driving systems.
