Self-play is not enough: human imitation improves self-play autonomy in the wild

TL;DR

Self-play RL makes driving policies robust but not human-like. SHIFT (Self-play with Human Imitation Fine-Tuning) pretrains with large-scale self-play, then fine-tunes on human driving logs in closed-loop log replay, using the same PPO loop with one added human-similarity reward and a KL anchor to the frozen self-play policy.

  • nuPlan Val14: beats self-play on 9 of 10 metrics across human-likeness (ADE −15%), safety (collisions −39%) and comfort (brake taps −31%, oscillations −53%), and every baseline on ADE, FDE, collisions and drivable-area violations.
  • Public roads: 2.6× fewer safety takeovers than self-play, and far fewer discomfort events flagged by the onboard test team (one every 13.1 miles vs. every 0.92). To our knowledge, this is the first on-road evaluation of self-play and self-play with human imitation.

How SHIFT works

Figure 1: SHIFT training pipeline.

Existing hybrid IL+RL methods follow an IL→RL pipeline: a pretrained imitation policy anchors self-play to the human driving distribution. That ordering makes iteration on the imitation side expensive, because adding a city, reweighting scenarios or refining a capability means rerunning self-play training. SHIFT reverses the order. We first pretrain a robust foundation policy with large-scale self-play, then fine-tune it on a modest amount of human driving data. Fine-tuning takes hours; self-play takes days.

Stage 1: self-play pretraining. Following the Gigaflow recipe, every agent in each parallel world is controlled by the same shared policy, with diversity induced by sampling a different reward-conditioning vector per agent. Training uses PPO with advantage filtering.

Stage 2: human imitation fine-tuning. The network, action space, optimizer and advantage filtering stay the same. Only four things change:

  • Closed-loop log replay. Each episode loads a real driving log. The ego is rolled out by the policy for 22 steps (∼6.6 s), while every other agent replays its logged human trajectory.
  • Fixed reward conditioning. All agents share one deployment-style conditioning vector, the same one used in nuPlan evaluation.
  • A human similarity reward. One extra term penalizes the Euclidean distance between the ego and the human expert at each step, with weight wsimilarity = 10−2.
  • A KL anchor. Without it, fine-tuning helps for a few hundred PPO cycles and then the policy forgets its self-play behavior. A KL loss against the frozen foundation policy, β · DKL(πθ ‖ πpre) with β = 10−2, allows long fine-tuning without losing it.

Results on nuPlan

We fine-tune on ∼92K scenes of human urban driving from 1,712 logs and evaluate on the held-out closed-loop nuPlan Val14 benchmark (1,115 scenarios, non-reactive agents). SHIFT outperforms the self-play baseline on 9 of 10 metrics, with the largest gains in human-likeness (ADE −15%, FDE −13%) and comfort (brake taps −31%, oscillations −53%). It is also safer: collisions −39%, drivable-area violations −32%, red-light violations −39%.

Baselines. Extra self-play gives only modest gains. BC only is comfortable but unsafe, with 14× more collisions, 24× more drivable-area violations and 17× more red-light violations than SHIFT. Naively combining BC+RL from scratch does worse still, which points to the role of self-play pretraining in this limited-data regime. BC-regularized self-play (TFISH and HR-PPO), the conventional IL→RL ordering, does not match SHIFT.

Fine-tuning for specific capabilities

Because fine-tuning is cheap, the same foundation policy can be specialized quickly. Fine-tuning on a dataset of nominal driving already improves substantially over self-play. A curated close-proximity dataset helps most in dense traffic, where the policy keeps up with expert speed and brakes less. Fine-tuning on both datasets in sequence gives the best of both: it is best on six of ten metrics overall, with collisions −44% and brake taps −42% relative to self-play.

Hyperparameter sweeps

Figure 6: Sweeps over training cycle (top), similarity weight (middle) and KL regularization (bottom) around the main configuration (1,500 cycles, wsimilarity = 10−2, β = 10−2).

Most of the human-likeness gain arrives in the first few hundred PPO cycles, while safety stays flat or improves across all 1,500. Raising the similarity weight from pure RL toward pure BC improves human-likeness and comfort, but safety worsens past 10−2. With too little KL, fine-tuning overwrites the self-play policy and behaves like BC only; with too much, the policy stops learning.

Driving on public roads

We deployed the self-play baseline and SHIFT, fine-tuned on either the nominal dataset or both datasets, on autonomous vehicles on public roads in Las Vegas, with a safety driver, on loops around the Motional Las Vegas office. Both SHIFT variants needed fewer safety takeovers and improved on every discretionary metric (stuck, slow driving, discomfort, bad driving). To our knowledge, these are the first on-vehicle results for a policy pretrained with self-play and fine-tuned with imitation learning.

Limitations

Non-reactive log replay limits episodes to 6.6 seconds; beyond that, replayed agents collide with the ego once its trajectory diverges from the log. Rolling out and supervising all agents during fine-tuning could remove this limit. Some edge cases involving yellow lights and stop signs degrade after fine-tuning, which suggests residual conflict between imitation and strict rule-following even with KL regularization. Our public-road tests also cover limited mileage, so further on-road evaluation is needed.
